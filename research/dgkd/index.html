Decoupled Gradient Knowledge Distillation

Gradient norm decay in logit distillation: a diagnosis, a coupling-based proposal, and what changed when we revisited the training setup.

01 / Motivation

Why inspect the learning signal?

Knowledge distillation (KD) has become a cornerstone technique for compressing large neural networks into smaller, deployable models. The canonical formulation — minimizing the KL divergence between teacher and student output distributions — is deceptively simple. Zhao et al. (2022) showed that this classical loss is actually a coupled formulation of two distinct objectives: Target Class Knowledge Distillation (TCKD), which transfers knowledge about prediction confidence on the correct class, and Non-Target Class Knowledge Distillation (NCKD), which transfers the "dark knowledge" embedded in the teacher's probability mass over wrong classes.

Their key finding was that NCKD is the dominant driver of distillation performance, yet classical KD suppresses it — the NCKD term is weighted by (1 − ptT), which shrinks precisely when the teacher is most confident and, by their argument, most informative. DKD addresses this by decoupling the two terms and introducing independent weights α and β.

As the student approaches the teacher’s distribution, KL-based distillation gradients shrink. This is expected near a matched distribution; the question is whether the signal becomes too weak before the student has learned the useful structure available from its teacher. Our project asks whether coupling the two gradient streams can change those training dynamics.

DKD background: Zhao et al., Decoupled Knowledge Distillation (2022).

02 / Theoretical Framework

Two perspectives on optimization

Our approach is grounded in two bodies of work from convex optimization and deep learning theory.

Neural Collapse and the MSE Loss Landscape. Zhou et al. (2022), “On the Optimization Landscape of Neural Collapse under MSE Loss: Global Optimality with Unconstrained Features,” analyze MSE under a simplified unconstrained-features model. Their work connects global minimizers to Neural Collapse and studies a rescaled MSE objective. It motivates examining loss geometry, but does not establish non-vanishing gradients or an optimal coupling strength for our distillation objective. MSE gradients can also vanish when the quantities being compared coincide.

Gradient Interaction in Multi-Objective Optimization. PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020) addresses conflicting task gradients, while GradNorm (Chen et al., 2018) adapts loss weights to balance training. These works motivate inspecting both gradient direction and magnitude. They do not imply that enforcing orthogonality between TCKD and NCKD is necessarily beneficial.

Background: Zhou et al. (2022), PCGrad, and GradNorm.

03 / Main Idea

A coupling term between gradients

We propose Decoupled Gradient Knowledge Distillation (DGKD), which augments the DKD loss with a coupling term that operates directly on the gradients of TCKD and NCKD with respect to the student logits. Rather than allowing the two loss components to interact only implicitly through shared parameters, we introduce an explicit regularizer that measures the divergence between their gradient signals.

Loss Formulation

The standard DKD loss is:

Baseline / DKD

LDKD = α LTCKD + β LNCKD

We extend this with a scaled MSE coupling term between the per-sample gradients of TCKD and NCKD with respect to the student's target-class and non-target-class logits respectively:

Compact notation: gt,i and gn,i denote the target and non-target logit-gradient quantities compared for sample i. Their shapes, alignment, and differentiation through the gradient computation must be specified by the implementation.

Proposed / DGKD

LDGKD = LDKD + ε RR = (1/n) ∑i=1n ‖gt,i − gn,i‖²
gt,i
The TCKD gradient with respect to the student’s target-class logit for sample i.
gn,i
The NCKD gradient with respect to the student’s non-target-class logits for sample i.
ε
The coupling scale, empirically set to 6 in the original exploration.

A note on the sign. With a positive coefficient and a minimized total loss, this term penalizes the squared difference between the compared gradient quantities. Adding it does not maximize that difference. Any observed increase in gradient norms must be explained through the full optimization dynamics, rather than inferred from the sign of this term alone.

Why scaled MSE specifically? In the original exploration, we also tried cosine similarity, KL divergence, and Wasserstein distance as coupling measures. MSE was the most stable in that setup. The scale ε controls the coupling’s contribution; ε = 6 was an empirical choice under those conditions, not a theoretically established optimum.

04 / Hypothesis

What the coupling might change

We hypothesize that the primary failure mode of logit-based KD — including DKD — is gradient norm collapse: as the student converges toward the teacher's distribution, the KL-based losses produce vanishingly small gradients, and the distillation signal effectively dies before the student has fully internalized the teacher's representational structure.

The working hypothesis is that coupling TCKD and NCKD can alter their gradient magnitudes during training. The additive squared-distance term alone does not supply a self-correcting lower bound on those magnitudes: it also becomes small when both compared gradients approach zero. Establishing a sustaining mechanism therefore requires evidence beyond the loss expression.

A secondary hypothesis is that this mechanism should be more effective in networks with sufficient representational capacity to maintain orthogonal gradient subspaces for TCKD and NCKD simultaneously. In low-capacity networks, forcing gradient diversity in a compressed parameter space may cause destructive interference rather than complementary learning.

05 / Empirical Observations

Magnitude and direction

Gradient Norm Dynamics

The central empirical finding supporting our hypothesis is the gradient norm dynamics throughout training. We track the gradient norms of TCKD and NCKD separately for both DKD and DGKD on ResNet50→ResNet18, CIFAR-100.

In DKD, both the target-class and non-target-class gradient norms decay together and collapse to near-zero by epoch 14 — the distillation signal effectively extinguishes. In DGKD, the target-class gradient norm starts significantly higher (~0.15 vs ~0.075) and remains elevated throughout training, sustaining a meaningful learning signal well into late training. The non-target norm also starts higher and decays more gradually.

These reported observations motivate the gradient-magnitude hypothesis. They do not, by themselves, establish improved final accuracy or a causal explanation for the difference.

Gradient Similarity

A natural alternative explanation would be that our coupling term works by enforcing gradient orthogonality between TCKD and NCKD — forcing the two loss components to provide genuinely distinct directions in parameter space. We tested this directly by measuring the cosine similarity between ∇LTCKD and ∇LNCKD across training for both methods.

The original analysis reports nearly indistinguishable cosine-similarity curves for DKD and DGKD, with near-orthogonal gradients in both cases. This makes a change in that measured similarity a less compelling explanation. It does not establish that every update direction is unchanged, or rule out other optimization effects.

For two nearly orthogonal gradient vectors, ‖gₜ − gₙ‖² ≈ ‖gₜ‖² + ‖gₙ‖². This identity helps interpret the measured quantities, but does not make a positive minimized distance penalty a gradient amplifier. The relationship between the regularizer, its derivatives, and the observed norms remains an open part of the analysis.

These gradient observations are reported in the original project write-up. The available training-accuracy figure below is a different experiment and does not directly plot those gradient norms or similarities.

06 / Revisiting the training setup

What changed under a better training setup?

Our original results were obtained under non-standard training conditions: approximately 8 epochs, without warmup, without the DKD paper's recommended LR schedule (step decay at epochs 150, 180, 210), and with unverified hyperparameters. Under these conditions, no model converges and the reported accuracy numbers are not meaningful baselines relative to the published DKD results.

When re-run under correct conditions — SGD with momentum 0.9, weight decay 5e-4, LR 0.05 with step decay, temperature 4, α = 1.0, β = 8.0, 20-epoch warmup — DKD outperforms our method on an initial sanity check. This raises three distinct possibilities:

1. The improvement was an artifact of unconverged training. Our coupling term may help in the early training regime where gradient norms are rapidly changing, but provide no benefit — or actively hurt — once training stabilizes under a proper schedule.

2. The scale parameter ε = 6 was tuned to the broken setup. The optimal coupling strength under short-horizon training may differ substantially from the optimal under full training. This is a likely explanation given that the parameter was found empirically without cross-validation under standard conditions.

3. The mechanism is real but operates at a different scale. The gradient norm sustenance we observe may still be occurring under proper training, but the benefit may be smaller than the noise introduced by a suboptimally-scaled coupling term.

Distinguishing between these requires a systematic seed experiment and scale sweep under proper training conditions, which is the current focus of ongoing work.

07 / Limitations

Where the evidence stops

Hyperparameter sensitivity. The coupling term introduces a single additional parameter ε, but performance degrades both above and below ε = 6 in our original experimental setup. This sensitivity to a single hyperparameter is the primary obstacle to claiming a robust mechanism rather than a tuned configuration.

No theoretical derivation of the optimal scale. We do not have an analytical characterization of why ε = 6 was favorable in the initial setup, or how the choice should vary with architecture, learning rate, or temperature. Connecting the motivating loss-landscape results to this gradient-coupling term remains open.

Capacity dependence. The original exploration reports failures on ShuffleNetV2 and MobileNet. Insufficient capacity to support the proposed gradient interaction is one hypothesis; those failures alone do not establish that explanation.

08 / Future Work

Experiments that would resolve the question

  • Theoretical derivation of the optimal scale. Connecting the optimal ε to properties of the training dynamics — loss magnitude, learning rate, temperature — would convert an empirical finding into a principled design choice and substantially strengthen the contribution.
  • Formal characterization of gradient norm collapse. Proving that KL-based distillation losses produce gradient norms that decay at a rate proportional to the KL divergence reduction, and showing that the coupling term provides a provable lower bound on gradient norm magnitude, would constitute the theoretical core of a publishable result.
  • Extension to broader architectures and datasets. Validation on ImageNet at full training, MS-COCO object detection, and architectures beyond the ResNet family is necessary before any general claims can be made.
  • Applying the coupling term to vanilla KD. If the mechanism is gradient norm sustenance rather than a DKD-specific fix, the coupling term should improve vanilla KD as well. This experiment would distinguish a general KD improvement from a DKD-specific one and is a strong test of the core hypothesis.
